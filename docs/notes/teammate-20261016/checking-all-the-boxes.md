

1) Checking all the boxes

---

In the canteen after his major classes, Vier’s phone buzzes with a message from home: \*“Kumusta ka na? Make sure to keep in touch\!”\* He smiles at the message, then proceeds to put down his phone. His mind wanders back to a list he could finish without anyone’s help: good grades, a good job, healthy relationships, enough money. Nobody ever handed him this list, yet he knows it by pure intuition, and somehow, it has already decided how far behind he is. 

Vier: 

* I don't really know what I'm passionate about or what I want to pursue, but I've always had this idea ingrained in my mind that a comfortable life is a happy life. I should get good grades, which would lead to better opportunities and, eventually, a good life. Once I'm financially stable and capable of supporting myself, pursuing happiness would become easier. Wala naman talagang nagturo o nagsabi sa akin na ganito dapat, so where did I get these ideas, and when did I start believing in them?

Wanting these things is fine. The trouble starts when they become the only acceptable way to live, with a timetable that makes any other pace feel like failure. Louis Althusser's concept of Ideological State Apparatuses (ISAs) helps explain how society reproduces these expectations through institutions such as schools, families, and media, leading us to recognize ourselves in the identities and aspirations they present (Althusser, 1971). This is reinforced by what Theodor Adorno describes as the “culture industry”, through which mass media repeatedly presents certain lifestyles and material comforts as desirable images of happiness (Adorno and Horkheimer, 2002). Over time, as Pierre Bourdieu's concept of “habitus” suggests, these expectations become ingrained through our upbringing and experiences, shaping what feels natural or possible to pursue (Bourdieu, 1977).

However, our opportunities are also shaped by the economic, cultural, and social capital available to us, meaning that not everyone has equal access to the resources needed to achieve these milestones Recognizing these influences does not mean Vier must abandon his aspirations. Rather, it gives him room to question them. He cannot entirely escape the society that shapes him, nor would he necessarily want to leave behind the people and possibilities he values. But he can begin to distinguish between what he genuinely wants and what he has learned to believe he should want. His agency lies in deciding what to keep, question, or change.
