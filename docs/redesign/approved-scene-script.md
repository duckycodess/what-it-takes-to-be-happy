## 1. One afternoon, three different answers

### s088 | No special occasion

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Imagine an ordinary afternoon with friends: a game, a few jokes, time to talk. Nothing gets finished. Nobody earns a grade.

TJ: Masaya lang tumawa with friends. Bakit kailangan may lesson or output pa?

Narrator: TJ values that kind of joy. Koj puts more weight on living well. Vier asks whether he has a say in his life. Their answers overlap, but they do not mean the same thing by happiness.

**Production:** A01, `group`. No interaction, caption or research citation. Copy is the approved sample, unchanged.

### s089 | Three answers

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Start with what each of us means.

Koj: Happiness is closer to living well than feeling good all the time. May purpose, relationships, choices, and room to grow.

TJ: A life na gusto kong balikan. May people, time, security, and space to enjoy things without making everything an achievement.

Vier: For me, it's my relationship with life. Hindi lahat okay, but I can still take part in it and make choices.

Narrator: Koj emphasizes fulfillment, TJ protects ordinary joy, and Vier emphasizes agency. One definition cannot silently replace the other two.

**Production:** A01, `group`. Dialogue order Koj, TJ, Vier. No interaction or caption. Member-definition answer; no new personal history.

### s090 | Where we're speaking from

**Reader copy**

Narrator: We are UP students discussing happiness through school, friends, hobbies and plans for the future. That position shapes this essay. We notice grades and productivity because they are close to us. We also have enough access to work together on a website.

Narrator: Examples that feel ordinary to us require things we can overlook: free time, transport, devices, somewhere to meet. We cannot treat that access as everyone's starting point. Our own habits belong inside the discussion, not outside it as neutral observations.

**Production:** `text`. No drawing or citation. Student standpoint comes from the group notes; do not invent anyone's income, household circumstances or hardship.

### s091 | One bad day

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Imagine someone tired after working on something they care about. The day felt frustrating. Looking back, they still value what they did and the people they did it with.

Narrator: Now imagine an easy afternoon that feels pleasant but leaves them dissatisfied with how their life is going. Neither example settles whether that person is happy. They show why we need more than one question.

Koj: Sadness or stress doesn't automatically mean a life is going badly.

Narrator: Being miserable isn't proof that you're living well either.

**Production:** A02, `single`. Illustrative example, not a study or member memory. No controls or caption.

## 2. Feeling good or living well?

### s092 | Two different questions

**Reader copy**

Narrator: “How did today feel?” and “How is my life going?” can produce different answers. Kahneman and Deaton separated everyday emotional well-being from life evaluation in their study of US respondents. The two measures related to income differently. [^01]

Narrator: That distinction helps us avoid treating every use of “happiness” as the same claim. Someone can enjoy a moment while wanting their circumstances to change. Someone can value their life while having a painful day. The study measures self-reports; it does not decide which definition we should live by.

**Production:** A02, `single`. No numerical scales or arrows in artwork. No control or caption.

### s093 | Living well takes more than a mood

**Reader copy**

Koj: Some things matter even while they're difficult. Studying can be stressful, but I can value what I'm learning more than an easier afternoon.

Narrator: Aristotle's account places happiness in virtuous activity across a complete life. It also acknowledges the need for external goods and resources. It is a philosophical account of living well, not evidence that difficult work makes people happy. [^02]

Narrator: Koj's view resembles that account. Our further question is about access: who has the time, support and resources to pursue the activities they value?

**Production:** A03, `single`; art follows Koj's dialogue. No control or caption. Chosen study is illustrative, not an actual dated event.

### s094 | Having a say

**Reader copy**

Vier: I don't need to call every part of my life good. What matters is whether I can still participate in it, instead of feeling that everything is decided for me.

Narrator: This makes agency central to Vier's definition. It includes choosing, responding and questioning what is expected of him. It does not require complete control or a life without regret.

Narrator: A choice can still be narrow. Choosing between two exhausting options is not the same as having room to build a life. We will need to look at what makes meaningful choices possible.

**Production:** A04, `single`; art follows Vier's dialogue. No depicted paths, map, escape portal or promised happy ending. No control or caption.

### s095 | Our definitions can become demands

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Living well sounds less demanding than being cheerful every day. But it can become another standard: always have a purpose, grow through every experience, make every choice meaningful.

Vier: Even “no regrets” can become an impossible requirement. Regret doesn't mean I have no say in what happens next.

Narrator: Our definitions need that check. If fulfillment becomes compulsory, an ordinary or unfinished life gets treated as failure. If agency means total independence, it ignores how much anyone depends on other people. A useful definition should help us understand a life, not grade every moment of it.

**Production:** `text`. No quoted theorist or research citation. No metric, score or evaluation widget.

### s096 | A hobby can offer several things

**Reader copy**

Narrator: A game with friends can offer enjoyment, a chance to improve, and time together. Those reasons can coexist. We do not have to decide that only one makes the activity worthwhile.

Narrator: Self-determination theory names autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs. It proposes that environments supporting them support well-being. [^03]

TJ: That gives names to things I value in hobbies: choosing to do them, learning something, and having people to share them with.

Narrator: This theory helps describe motivation. It does not require every hobby to produce growth or explain everything we mean by happiness.

**Production:** A05, `single`. No toggle: paragraph already explains all three needs. No caption or score.

## 3. Must joy be productive?

### s097 | Chosen challenge, forced hardship

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Difficulty is not valuable by itself. A chosen project can feel demanding and still be fulfilling. An obligation that leaves someone exhausted can feel demanding without giving them that choice or reward.

TJ: I can choose a hard challenge because I want it. I don't want that used to tell someone their hardship is good for them.

Narrator: Koj's defense of meaningful effort does not require romanticizing suffering. Ask what the person values, whether they had a real choice, and what support they have. The same amount of effort can mean very different things under different conditions.

**Production:** A03 and A06, `pair`. View labels exactly `Chosen effort` then `Required work`. A06 depicts a fictional person; never assign the obligation to Koj. No control or caption.

### s098 | Rest does not need to pay us back

**Reader copy**

TJ: Sometimes I want to play or rest because I enjoy it. Hindi kailangan may lesson, improvement, or output lagi.

Narrator: If rest is defended only because it makes us productive afterward, work still decides its value. We think rest and play can matter without that return.

Narrator: Haselberger's personal essay describes building a life around work until little remains when work ends. [^04] Her reflection adds a perspective to our argument. The balance between work and enjoyment still depends on what someone values and what their life allows.

**Production:** A07, `single`; art follows TJ's dialogue. No relaxation instructions, timer, reward or caption.

### s099 | Who enjoys the afternoon?

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Bentham evaluates actions through the pleasure and pain they produce: their intensity, duration, likelihood and other consequences, including how many people are affected. [^05]

Narrator: Applied to our afternoon, the question becomes more than “Did I have fun?” Who had time to join? Who organized it? Did someone feel left out? These are our questions using his framework, not Bentham's account of this scene.

Narrator: This lens helps us notice consequences beyond ourselves. It also leaves a problem: pleasures that last equally long can matter differently to different people. Counting time alone cannot settle their value.

**Production:** A01, `group`. No invented numerical happiness calculator or score. No control or caption.

### s100 | Who ranks pleasures?

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Mill argues that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. To judge between them, he appeals to people acquainted with both. [^06]

TJ: Pero who gets to be that judge? Taste, education and wanting approval can shape what we call a better activity.

Narrator: That is TJ's objection, not a concession from Mill. Studying and gaming can each involve effort, imagination or company. Neither label tells us everything about what someone gets from the activity.

Narrator: We can compare experiences without pretending the person making the comparison stands outside class, upbringing or preference.

**Production:** A03 and A05, `pair`. View labels `Studying` then `Playing`. Neither gets a medal, higher position or happier face as a verdict. No control or caption.

### s101 | Needs are not a rigid staircase

**Reader copy**

Narrator: The familiar needs pyramid can suggest that people must finish one level before another matters. Maslow's own paper describes a less rigid order: people can be partly satisfied at several levels, and higher needs emerge gradually. The pyramid is not his original figure. [^07]

Narrator: Someone facing insecurity can still value affection, dignity or learning. Their joy is not fake because another need remains unmet.

Narrator: The reverse also matters: finding connection does not remove the need for food, safety or rest. Needs can coexist. One fulfilled need does not excuse neglecting another.

**Production:** `text`. No pyramid, percentages or needs-completion meter. No control or caption.

### s102 | No winning activity

**Reader copy**

Narrator: We do not settle this by choosing between pleasure and fulfillment. Koj values living well, TJ wants room for ordinary enjoyment, and Vier asks about participation and choice.

Narrator: An afternoon can offer several of these at once. Another activity can matter for different reasons. Even the same person can need challenge one day and rest the next.

Narrator: Our conclusion is that happiness cannot be reduced to the easiest, most productive or most meaningful activity. The next question is how we learned to call certain lives and activities better in the first place.

**Production:** A01, `group`. No control or caption.

## 4. Whose idea of happiness are we following?

### s103 | The expected life

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Good grades, a stable career, a relationship, an income that keeps growing. These can be things someone wants. They can also become the only accepted picture of a successful life.

Vier: I want to ask which parts I want, and which parts I follow because they're treated as the correct way to live.

Narrator: Our class discussion of habitus helps frame how surroundings can make certain choices feel natural. Here we use that class lens, not a passage we read in its original formulation. It asks us to examine learned expectations without assuming everyone absorbs them identically.

**Production:** A08, `single`. Browser labels `Grades`, `Career`, `Relationship`, `Income` beside their corresponding blank props; no sequential milestones, universal timeline or counter. No control or caption.

### s104 | What does the purchase give me?

**Reader copy**

TJ: I'm not above consumer happiness. I like things too. I want to ask what the purchase gives me: a hobby, convenience, a way to connect, or pressure to keep up.

Narrator: Van Boven and Gilovich found greater reported and anticipated happiness from experiential than material purchases in their studies. [^08] That finding does not make objects shallow. A device can make a hobby possible. An experience can arrive with debt or status pressure.

Narrator: Those are our qualifications. The question is what the purchase does in someone's life, including its cost, rather than whether its category sounds more meaningful.

**Production:** A09a, `single`; art follows TJ's dialogue. No price, brand or product recommendation. No control or caption.

### s105 | One phone, different uses

**Reader copy**

Narrator: The same phone can let someone talk with a friend and compare their life with a stream of milestones. “Stop using your phone” treats those uses as interchangeable.

Narrator: Verduyn and colleagues' review reported different associations for active and passive social-network use. It proposed connection and comparison as different mechanisms. [^09] A separate study found a very small average association between adolescent technology use and well-being. [^10]

Narrator: Neither finding gives a rule for every person or platform. An average can miss particular harms, and a label such as “active” does not tell us whether an exchange felt supportive. Look at the use and its context.

**Production:** A09a/A09b, `single`, optional art-state control. Default `Connection`; second state `Comparison`. Native buttons with those exact labels and selected state exposed via aria-pressed; Enter/Space activate. All paragraphs stay visible in both states. No extra state narration. Reduced motion switches still images. Continuous/print show both drawings with those labels, in that order.

### s106 | Pressure to be cheerful

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Being told to cheer up can feel different from being allowed to explain what is wrong. Encouragement becomes pressure when sadness itself is treated as failure.

Narrator: Bastian and colleagues studied perceived expectations not to feel sad. Across their studies, those expectations were associated with, and experimentally followed by, more negative emotion and lower well-being. The Australian and Japanese samples did not respond identically. [^11]

Narrator: This is not an argument against cheerful company. It is a reason to ask what encouragement permits: can the person still describe their experience, or must they hide it to be accepted?

**Production:** A10, `single`. Browser dialogue label beside the fictional encouraging friend exactly `Cheer up!`; this is illustrative speech, not a quotation from the study. No control or caption.

### s107 | Joy does not cancel the problem

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Someone can appreciate a meal with friends and remain angry about conditions making everyday life harder. Gratitude does not require calling those conditions acceptable.

Narrator: Nor can we assume people will adjust to anything. Diener, Lucas and Scollon revised the idea of one fixed happiness baseline: people differ in adaptation, and their set points can change under some conditions. [^12]

TJ: Small joys count. Pero they shouldn't become a reason to tell people they don't need more time, security or support.

Narrator: Resilience can describe how someone keeps going. It does not decide what burdens they should have to carry.

**Production:** A11, `single`. No forced contrast between a smile and a “true” unhappy face. No control or caption.

### s108 | Choosing what to keep

**Reader copy**

Vier: I can't find a version of myself that was never shaped by society. I can still question what I've learned and decide what to keep.

Narrator: Agency is negotiation here, not escape. We can accept some expectations, reject others, and change our minds. The standards used to choose are also learned, so reflection does not place us outside social influence.

Narrator: That does not make choice meaningless. It means our definition of happiness has to include room to question expectations and people with whom we can discuss them. That brings us to relationships.

**Production:** A04, `single`; art follows Vier's dialogue. No society-free avatar, path or transformed “authentic” person. No control or caption.

## 5. When does connection become pressure?

### s109 | Ordinary care

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Remembering what a friend said. Making room for someone at lunch. Noticing when they have gone quiet. Care often happens through small acts, not a single moment that proves a relationship's value.

Narrator: The publisher descriptions of Lambek's and Das's books on ordinary ethics draw attention to ethics in everyday action and attentiveness to others. We use those descriptions as an entry point, not as passages from the books. [^13] [^14]

Narrator: The way people treat one another can make ordinary life easier to return to. Those same routines can also exclude or exhaust someone.

**Production:** A12, `group`. Browser names above the seated TJ/Koj/Vier heads. No control or caption.

### s110 | Acquaintances can matter too

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Connection does not have to mean a best friend or partner. A familiar person at class or a brief conversation can also make someone feel less alone.

Narrator: Sandstrom and Dunn found that people reported greater happiness and belonging on days with more interactions with weak ties. Their first study involved students. This is a daily association, not proof that adding acquaintances causes happiness. [^15]

Narrator: We take it as a reason to notice ordinary contact, without turning sociability into a quota. A person does not fail at happiness because they want fewer conversations or more time alone.

**Production:** `text`. No friend count, social score or invented encounter attributed to a member.

### s111 | Sharing a meal

**Reader copy**

Narrator: A meal can give people time together without requiring a major event. The World Happiness Report's 2025 chapter on shared meals found an association with higher life evaluations in data spanning 142 countries. Its authors do not establish which causes which. [^16]

Narrator: The number of shared meals also cannot tell us whether the company was caring, tense or compulsory. In our opening, the value lies in enjoying time together, not meeting a meal target.

Narrator: Connection needs room to happen. Food, time and a place to sit are part of that room, not background details.

**Production:** A12, `group`. No country ranking, meal counter or guaranteed effect. No control or caption.

### s112 | Who keeps the relationship going?

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Someone remembers dates, checks schedules, prepares food and follows up. Friendship can be meaningful while requiring work. The Working Mom Chronicles describes that effort in a personal reflection. [^17]

Narrator: Giving can be rewarding too. Dunn, Aknin and Norton found greater reported happiness from spending on others across their designs, including an experiment. [^18] They studied money people could allocate, not an obligation to keep giving until nothing remains.

Narrator: Our question is whether care is shared and sustainable. Appreciating generosity should not hide who supplies the time, money or effort.

**Production:** A13, `single`. Fictional organizer beside meal dishes and a blank scheduling phone; no member assigned the burden. No control or caption.

### s113 | Boundaries belong in connection

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Kapwa describes a shared inner self and relations with others within Sikolohiyang Pilipino. We draw this account from Bautista and Aguilar's discussion of earlier work. It is a secondary source, not a test of what every Filipino believes. [^19]

Narrator: Recognizing others does not mean agreeing to every demand. A relationship can allow someone to say no, rest, or spend time alone without making them prove they care.

Narrator: That is our position: connection and boundaries can support each other. Solitude is not automatically loneliness, and belonging is not automatically freedom from pressure.

**Production:** A14, `single`. Relaxed fictional Bob reading alone; no lonely expression imposed. No control or caption.

## 6. Who has room to choose?

### s114 | The same invitation, different options

**Reader copy**

Narrator: “Come play after class” sounds like the same invitation to everyone. Imagine one person has a free afternoon. Another has a shift, a long trip home or someone depending on their care.

Narrator: Both can want to join. Their willingness does not give them the same options. These are fictional examples, not claims about any group member's circumstances.

Narrator: Advice to make time becomes thin if it never asks what already claims that time. To understand whether our definition of happiness is achievable, we have to look at schedules, resources and responsibilities as well as preferences.

**Production:** A15a/A15b, `single`, optional art-state control. Default label `Time available`; second label `Time claimed`. Same fictional Bob identity in both. Native buttons/aria-pressed/Enter/Space as s105. All copy stays visible. Continuous/print display both drawings and labels in order. No automatic animation or disabled Next.

### s115 | Buying time moves the work

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Whillans and colleagues found that paying for time-saving services was associated with greater life satisfaction. Their field experiment also found greater reported happiness after a time-saving purchase than a material one. The samples came from four high-income countries, none of them the Philippines. [^20]

Narrator: Buying time also requires money. And the task moves to someone else; it does not disappear. The study's finding does not tell us whether that person's work is secure or fairly paid.

Narrator: We need both questions: what gives one person more time, and what conditions that creates for the person doing the work?

**Production:** A16, `pair`. View labels `Time gained` then `Work performed`. These are fictional people, not an experiment illustration. No control or caption.

### s116 | There is no universal price of happiness

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Income matters partly because it changes what someone can afford and what pressures they face. But “How much is enough?” needs a place, a time, a household and a definition of happiness.

Narrator: Kahneman and Deaton's 2010 US study distinguished life evaluation from emotional well-being. Later, Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers found that the relationship between income and emotional well-being differed across the happiness distribution; the earlier flattening claim was overstated. [^01] [^21]

Narrator: These are still US, correlational findings. They cannot supply a peso threshold for us, or prove that money guarantees happiness. A headline number conceals the circumstances it measures.

**Production:** `text`. No money recommendation, threshold conversion, unsupported chart or income slider.

### s117 | Joy in hardship is real

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Palanca-Tan's study surveyed 522 respondents in Koronadal City in 2019. Their mean self-rated happiness was 6.75 out of 10 despite a poverty incidence above the national figure. This was one city, not a national sample. [^22]

Narrator: The finding matters because deprivation does not erase people's enjoyment or relationships. It cannot tell us that deprivation is harmless. A happiness score is not an account of every pressure in a person's life.

TJ: Saying someone can find joy with little should not become “then they don't need anything to change.” Those are different claims.

**Production:** `text`. No generic poor-but-cheerful figure, poverty aesthetic or threshold graphic.

### s118 | Having a resource, being able to use it

**Reader copy**

Narrator: A resource is not the same as a real opportunity. Two people can receive the same bus fare while having different access to usable transport, free time or a safe route.

Narrator: Robeyns and Byskov's account of the capability approach distinguishes resources from what someone is able to do and be. People differ in how they can turn resources into valued activities. [^23]

Narrator: Applied here, access means more than being told an activity is available. Can the person realistically reach it, participate, and choose whether it suits them?

**Production:** `text`. Concrete bus-fare example is our application, not a reported experiment. No control or caption.

### s119 | Name the barrier

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Galtung's idea of structural violence examines avoidable gaps between what people could realize and what social arrangements allow them to realize. What counts as avoidable is itself a difficult judgment. [^24]

Narrator: Name the barrier, the people affected and the mechanism. Imagine a space advertised as open to everyone that people cannot reach or enter accessibly. A design decision can restrict opportunity without anyone directly confronting the excluded person.

Narrator: Applying this idea to an actual place requires evidence about the barrier and its effects. Calling an arrangement structural violence should explain a mechanism, not replace the explanation.

**Production:** `text`. No villain figure, measured campus claim or universal “system causes unhappiness” arrow.

### s120 | Poverty is also about relationships of power

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Mosse argues that durable poverty is shaped by economic and political relations and social categorization, including power over the terms of debate. His work is grounded in adivasi and dalit communities in India; bringing that lens here is our application. [^25]

Narrator: It pushes us to ask who sets schedules, allocates resources and gets heard when conditions are challenged. Someone's hardship is not always an isolated shortage they can solve alone.

Narrator: We also question treating every problem as a personal purchase or self-improvement task. Harvey's publisher description identifies market exchange as an ethic in neoliberalism; our connection to productivity and wellness advice is ours, not a claim from an unread chapter. [^26]

**Production:** `text`. No invented quotations, campus policy data or geographic equivalence.

### s121 | Options do not guarantee feelings

**Reader copy**

Narrator: More time, security and access can make valued activities possible. They cannot guarantee affection, fulfillment or a particular mood. That limit does not make those conditions irrelevant.

Narrator: It also applies to our own work. Making a website is an option we have, and it encourages us to imagine technical answers. An app can help people coordinate. It cannot by itself shorten a workday, pay a fare, or make a relationship caring.

Narrator: Our conclusion is that happiness involves experience and opportunity. Looking at either one alone misses part of the question.

**Production:** `text`. No app mockup, “solution” animation or happiness guarantee.

## 7. What should change?

### s122 | Possible, but unequally sustained

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Is our definition of happiness achievable in today's society? Yes, but unequally. People can enjoy things, value their lives and exercise agency under difficult conditions. The opportunity to sustain those experiences is not evenly available.

Narrator: For us, the answer cannot be only “change your mindset” or only “wait until society changes.” Personal choices matter within conditions people do not choose.

Narrator: Changes should expand time, security, access and participation without prescribing one happy life. That is the direction we defend, not a promise that one policy will make everyone feel good.

**Production:** `text`. Explicit third assignment answer. No universal happiness formula.

### s123 | Changes within reach

**Reader copy**

Narrator: At a personal level, we can notice which activities we value, keep room for rest, and question whether a purchase or goal serves us. In relationships, we can share planning and care instead of leaving them to the person who always remembers.

Narrator: Those are practices, not duties to become happier. Some people have little room to act on them. Failing to make time does not prove someone chose badly when obligations already fill it.

Narrator: Personal practices belong alongside changes to conditions. They become blame when offered as substitutes for support, safer work or a manageable schedule.

**Production:** `text`. No self-help checklist, reader score, compulsory commitment or medical advice.

### s124 | Make room on campus

**Reader copy**

Narrator: A school or community can make ordinary connection easier through places people can reach, sit and stay without needing to buy something. Schedules can also leave time for rest and participation instead of treating every gap as wasted capacity.

Narrator: These are proposals. Their design should include the people who use and maintain the space: students with different schedules and access needs, staff, cleaners and others affected.

Narrator: A free space is not accessible just because it has no entrance fee. Opening hours, transport, physical access and the cost of keeping it usable still matter.

**Production:** A17, `single`. Fictional proposed campus space; no named actual location. No control or caption.

### s125 | Conditions institutions can change

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Institutions can act on conditions individuals cannot fix alone: security of work, reasonable demands on time, usable public services and access to resources. We support examining changes in these areas because they affect people's real options.

Narrator: That does not identify a policy already proven to increase happiness. We have not evaluated a specific intervention. A proposal needs evidence about who benefits, who is excluded, what it costs and whether people can use it.

Narrator: Listening to people affected is part of the change itself. They should have a say in what counts as an improvement, rather than receive someone else's definition of a good life.

**Production:** `text`. Normative direction, not policy-effect claim. No invented budget, logo or success rate.

### s126 | Who benefits, who pays?

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Our proposals need the same scrutiny as anyone else's. Longer opening hours can give students more time together. If the change adds unpaid work or shifts costs onto staff, its benefit is being bought at someone else's expense.

Narrator: Bentham's attention to everyone affected helps us ask about those consequences. Mill's concern with the value of experiences reminds us not to count every benefit as interchangeable. Neither framework settles who has authority to decide. [^05] [^06]

Narrator: The people doing the work need to be included in that decision. “More people can enjoy this” is the start of an assessment, not its conclusion.

**Production:** A18, `pair`. Exact labels `Students using the space` then `Staff maintaining it`. No control, score, quantified balance or caption.

### s127 | Test a proposal before claiming success

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Consider the proposed free campus space. Start by asking intended users and staff what currently prevents access. Set hours and maintenance arrangements with them, including paid work and usable access. Then check whether the space is being used and who still cannot use it.

Narrator: Attendance alone would not prove happiness increased. People may value the space for different reasons, or find that it fails to meet their needs.

Narrator: If the proposal creates a new burden or excludes the people it aimed to serve, the design should change. Testing it means allowing that conclusion, not collecting approval for a decision already made.

**Production:** A17, `single`. No “completed experiment” marks, collected results or invented before/after chart. No control or caption.

## 8. What can we conclude?

### s128 | What we agree on

**Reader copy**

Narrator: We agree that happiness is not constant cheerfulness, one milestone, or proof that a person optimized their life. We also agree that pleasure in hardship is real without making hardship acceptable.

Narrator: Koj's living well, TJ's life worth returning to, and Vier's agency remain distinct. Their overlap is room to do things they value and sustain relationships that matter to them.

Narrator: That room includes social and material conditions. Happiness is personal in how it is experienced, but the chances to pursue it are not produced by the individual alone.

**Production:** `text`. Synthesis without manufacturing a single shared definition.

### s129 | What constitutes happiness?

**Reader copy**

Narrator: Our answer includes pleasure, fulfillment, rest, relationships and agency. Time, security and access shape which of these people can pursue and sustain. None replaces all the others.

Narrator: This is not a checklist every person must complete. Connection can include solitude and boundaries. Fulfillment can include difficulty without excusing forced hardship. Rest need not become preparation for more work.

Narrator: The useful question is how these things fit into a person's life and what prevents them from pursuing what matters. That keeps our answer concrete without turning it into a universal score.

**Production:** `text`. Explicit second assignment answer. No pictorial checklist or universal needs diagram.

### s130 | Where we still differ

**Reader copy**

Koj: I still put more weight on living well than on making life easy. Some of what I value takes effort.

TJ: I still want to protect enjoyment that doesn't need to become a lesson or achievement.

Vier: I still ask whether I have a say in the life being called happy. A good-looking life isn't enough if I feel I can't participate in it.

Narrator: These differences matter. We do not resolve them by assigning everyone the same balance. They help us question whose values are being used when someone tells another person how to be happy.

**Production:** `text`. No vote, member ranking, author portraits or forced consensus animation.

### s131 | Turn the question back on us

**Reader copy**

Narrator: We can turn meaningful work into a standard everyone must meet. We can defend ordinary joy while overlooking who makes it possible. We can praise agency while imagining choices wider than the ones people have.

Narrator: Our student position makes school pressure and future plans easy to notice. Our access to friends, devices and time can make other barriers easier to miss. Our interest in building a website also makes technological answers attractive.

Narrator: Naming these biases does not remove them. It tells us where our argument needs correction: when our own ordinary life is being treated as the baseline for everyone else.

**Production:** `text`. Inward reflection belongs in the argument, not an added apology footer.

### s132 | Back to the afternoon

**Reader copy**

Narrator: The afternoon still counts. Its jokes and company do not need a grade, output or life-changing lesson to matter.

Narrator: But it also takes time to spare, people able to join, and somewhere to stay. Happiness is possible in the society we have. Making more room for it means protecting ordinary joy while changing the conditions that restrict choice and connection.

Narrator: What would give you, and the people around you, more room for a life worth returning to?

**Production:** A01, `group`. This is the sole closing reflection question. No input field, saved response, score or additional reflection prompts. End controls `Read again` and `Sources & Credits`.
