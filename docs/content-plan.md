# Content plan

The six chapters organize shared questions. Each may contain many short beats, comparisons, dialogues, visual situations, and reflective pauses. No chapter is reserved for one member and no storytelling method is compulsory throughout.

This is a coverage and drafting plan, not final scene prose. Claims below describe the current group notes and require the source audit in [research.md](research.md) before publication.

## 1. What counts as happiness?

Open through everyday snapshots of play, meaningful challenge, and rest. Different emotions can coexist across the same day. Let the three avatars interpret those situations differently.

- Koj: happiness as living well, with meaningful relationships, purpose, autonomy, progress, leisure, and security. Constant positive emotion is unrealistic.
- TJ: distinguish feeling good now, life satisfaction, and becoming someone valued; preserve ordinary joy and a life worth returning to.
- Vier: happiness as a relationship with life and capacity to act; dissatisfaction, regret, and uncertainty can coexist with it.
- Examine whether "contentment," "no regrets," or "meaning" imposes another metric. Do not claim the group has resolved these definitions.

Lenses: Aristotle/eudaimonia; different well-being measures; Vier's agency questions. Current sources: R01, R02. Connect the definitions to what readers notice rather than begin with a dictionary lecture.

## 2. Must enjoyment become meaningful or productive?

Keep the strongest versions of Koj's meaningful difficulty and TJ's defense of pleasure without achievement. Chosen challenge and forced hardship differ.

- Basketball, co-op games, ordinary hangouts, coding, and learning can mix enjoyment, effort, competence, company, and frustration.
- Ask who ranks studying above gaming, and what class, taste, education, or approval contributes to that judgment.
- Rest can have value without being a tool for tomorrow's productivity. Examine hobbies becoming side hustles, exercise becoming a score, or outings becoming content.
- Needs affect attention without establishing that people cannot have relationships or meaning while survival needs remain unmet.

Lenses: Bentham, Mill, Aristotle; autonomy/competence/relatedness; Maslow as a lens to examine rather than a rigid diagram. Current sources: R01, R03, R05–R08. End with negotiation among pleasure, meaning, growth, and rest, not a winning activity.

## 3. Whose definition are we following?

Use Vier's socially learned happiness templates alongside TJ's ambivalence about consumption, optimization, comparison, and online life.

- Success markers such as grades, university, relationships, and money may help people or become the only acceptable path.
- Social influence does not automatically make a desire fake. Ask which influences someone wants to retain, without inventing a completely society-free self.
- Objects and experiences overlap: a device may enable a valued hobby; a trip may bring debt or stress. Keep TJ's own consumer interests visible in the reflection.
- Online communities, games, friendship, and learning can matter. Comparison pressure can coexist with connection; reject an automatic "social media bad" verdict.
- Shared milestones conceal different circumstances. "Don't compare" can ease pressure without removing material barriers.
- Gratitude, resilience, anger, and desire for change can coexist. Compulsory positivity can silence difficult feelings. Adaptation is not a guarantee that every life returns to one fixed baseline.

Lenses: social upbringing/habitus as actually noted in class, socially produced expectations, agency, compulsory positivity. Current sources: R04, R22–R26; R29 where the connection is explicitly the group's interpretation. Do not silently attribute Vier's argument to a theorist he did not cite.

## 4. How do relationships help, and when does care become pressure?

Move from the joy of company toward the ordinary work of belonging and its limits.

- Sharing ordinary thoughts matters beyond achievements or romantic partnership. Repeated hangouts can matter without becoming milestones.
- Promises, listening, inclusion, remembering, and everyday replies carry ethical significance; context matters for exclusion or careless jokes.
- Weak ties and welcoming spaces widen the question beyond finding a best friend. Do not treat mortality findings as measurements of happiness.
- Kapwa offers a relational lens; collectivity may also bring conformity, exclusion, or unequal expectations.
- Care takes time and labor. Show boundaries, solitude, autonomy, and the question of whose effort sustains connection.

Lenses: ordinary ethics (Lambek/Das), kapwa/pakikipagkapwa, care, boundaries. Current sources: R14–R21. External personal accounts remain external accounts, not member biography or population proof.

## 5. Who has room for happiness?

Reveal the conditions behind a previously enjoyable situation. The same invitation can depend on money, energy, transport, schedules, care obligations, safety, and usable access.

- Money can mean necessities, an emergency buffer, a working device, options, or ability to refuse harm. "Enough" depends on responsibilities and costs.
- Income findings depend on population and well-being measure. Do not introduce a universal salary threshold or convert US findings into a Filipino happiness price.
- Joy in hardship is real; it does not justify poverty. The Koronadal study is local, not nationally representative evidence by default.
- Time-saving purchases raise affordability and labor questions. Outsourcing work transfers a task to someone rather than erasing it.
- Free calendar space may contain commuting, chores, care work, exhaustion, and requirements. Nominal services or public spaces may still be unusable because of hours, access, or safety.
- Relational poverty, structural barriers, market-centered responsibility, and real freedom examine mechanisms beyond mindset. Explain the actual barrier and affected people when using structural violence.
- Student, worker, caregiver, breadwinner, disability, solitude, and support situations illustrate different options, without predetermining anyone's feelings.
- Examine the group's UP/student standpoint and its limits. An app cannot automatically supply time, fare, care, or safety.

Lenses: needs, agency/capabilities, structural violence, relational poverty, neoliberalism. Current sources: R02, R09–R13, R27–R30. Keep important measurement, local-sample, causality, and labor limitations visible.

## 6. What could change, and what do we still disagree about?

Answer the assignment's present-society and change questions explicitly. Happiness is possible in different ways and unequally sustained; reducing barriers creates opportunities without guaranteeing feelings.

- Personal practices: room for leisure, everyday care, questioning inherited desires, permission for difficult emotions. They are possibilities, not blame rules.
- Campus/community: affordable and accessible gatherings, places to sit, welcoming groups, circumstances-sensitive support.
- Institutions: work predictability, manageable schedules, transport, healthcare, housing, and usable services. The current notes propose directions, not evaluated local policies.
- For each proposal ask who benefits, who bears costs, who might be excluded, and what evidence would support the change. Higher average happiness alone does not settle those questions.
- Preserve unresolved tensions: meaning versus pleasure, socially shaped desires versus agency, care versus boundaries, gratitude versus justified anger, and the group's risk of universalizing its own definition.
- Revisit an earlier ordinary joy with its conditions now visible. This may echo an earlier scene without imposing a traveler or fixed physical route.

Lenses: agency, real freedom, everyday ethics, structural conditions, utilitarian benefits/costs where supported by class notes. Current sources: relevant audited material from earlier chapters, especially R27–R30. New policy-specific claims need their own evidence.
